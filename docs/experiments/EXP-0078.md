# EXP-0078 — What a VRAM budget buys on a hybrid CPU/GPU decode

**Status:** PASSED (measurement tool + curve)
**Code:** `tools/kata_place.cpp`, `Model::routing_record_start/take` in
`include/kata/model.h` + `src/model.cpp`

## 1. Problem

The engine targets consumer PCs, which usually have *some* GPU — 4 or 8 GB. The
model does not fit there (15 GB), so a full GPU path is not the question. The
question is what partial residency is worth, and whether the answer is
predictable from routing data alone — before any HIP integration work.

The architectural constraint that decides everything: **weights cannot migrate
per token.** PCIe moves ~16 GB/s; one expert is 6.12 MB and a token activates
152 of them, so a single expert crossing the bus costs 0.4 ms. Therefore an
expert must live permanently on one device and the token routes to it.

## 2. Implementation

`Model::routing_record_start()` / `routing_record_take()` record, for every
decode step, the experts that one token chose in each of the 38 MoE layers —
`[step][moe_layer][n_expert_used]`. This is a per-token record, not the pooled
heatmap of EXP-0053: pooled counts cannot say whether a token's 152 selections
are independently spread (predictable, low variance) or clumped (one unlucky
token stalls the schedule).

`kata_place` then prices a residency policy from that record: fill the budget
with fixed weights + KV first (read every token, so worth more than any expert),
then experts ranked by their own layer's activation count. Expert bytes are
measured from the GGUF (`ffn_gate_exps` + `ffn_up_exps` + `ffn_down_exps` of
`blk.2`, divided by `expert_count`) rather than from assumed bit widths — the
EXP-0071 error of charging q4_K to `ffn_down_exps` cost 24%.

## 3. Results

LFM2.5-24B-A2B at a 2236-token prefix, 192 captured decode steps, GPU assumed
at **280 GB/s** (a consumer card; this machine's 764 is not representative),
CPU at the measured 73.5 GB/s:

| VRAM | experts resident | CPU MB/token | CPU ms | tok/s | spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (today) | 0 | 2208 | 30.04 | 33.3 | — |
| 1 GB | 0 | 2208 | 30.04 | 33.3 | — |
| 2 GB | 117 | 899 | 12.23 | 81.8 | 3% |
| 4 GB | 444 | 783 | 10.65 | 93.9 | 6% |
| 6 GB | 771 | 666 | 9.06 | 110.4 | 11% |
| 8 GB | 1098 | 555 | 7.55 | 132.4 | 12% |
| 12 GB | 1752 | 292 | 3.98 | 219.1 | 17% |
| 16 GB | 1950 | 227 | 3.09 | 219.1 | 21% |

**1 GB buys nothing** (1278 MB of fixed weights + KV do not fit) and **past
~12 GB the curve is flat** — the CPU side is no longer the critical path; the
GPU's own read of the 1278 MB fixed set is, at 4.6 ms.

At 400 GB/s the same curve reaches 313 tok/s at 16 GB, and 12 GB gives 248 — so
the ceiling moves with the card, but the *shape* does not.

## 4. The variance question, answered

"Spread" is max per-token CPU bytes vs the mean over the captured steps:

| residents | spread |
|---|---|
| 2 GB | 3% |
| 8 GB | 12% |
| 16 GB | 21% |

So the clumping is **small but not negligible**, and it grows as the resident
set shrinks — exactly backwards from what a naïve variance argument predicts.
The mechanism: with few experts resident, the CPU's share is a small fraction of
152 selections, so the binomial spread is a large *relative* share of a small
number. At 2 GB the CPU sees 899 MB mean and the worst token 3% more, which is
noise. At 16 GB the CPU sees 227 MB mean and the worst 21% more = 48 MB extra =
0.65 ms, in a 3.1 ms token. That is a real tail but not a schedule-breaking one.

## 5. Conclusion

Keep the tool. Two conclusions worth acting on:

1. **The first 2 GB is the whole prize on a small card.** Going 0 → 2 GB is
   33.3 → 81.8 tok/s (2.5x); 2 → 8 GB adds only another 1.6x. A hybrid that
   only takes fixed weights + KV to the GPU captures most of the achievable win
   and needs no expert placement, no per-expert residency policy, and no
   device-aware router — it is a strictly simpler product.
2. **Expert placement pays from 2 GB upward but degrades gracefully.** No cliff
   anywhere, so an approximate policy (e.g. per-layer top-N by heat, ignoring
   exact budgets) loses little.

Not established: these are **modal** numbers from a traffic model with one
synchronisation per MoE layer assumed free. The real decode has a dependency
chain — layer *i*'s expert output feeds layer *i+1*'s router — so the CPU and
GPU shares of different layers cannot overlap freely. That is the next
experiment, and it is the one place the 2.5x could shrink.

## 6. Bugs found

Both were silent-wrong-answer bugs in the measurement, not in the engine:

- `n_moe_layer` was counted next to the metadata reads and again right after
  `layers.resize()`, i.e. **before** `L.moe` was ever assigned. It stayed 0, so
  the routing record never grew and the tool reported "no routing recorded".
- `resident[]` was indexed by the bare expert id instead of
  `[layer][expert]`, making every expert look resident. The curve was flat at
  the all-CPU row for every budget — a 0-% speedup table that looks like a
  finding rather than a bug.
- The armed flag: gating the record on `!rec.empty()` meant it never grew, since
  it starts empty by definition.
